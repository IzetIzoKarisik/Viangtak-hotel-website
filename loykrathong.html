Loi Krathong Sai · Tak, Thailand

A River of a Thousand Lights

Every November, the Ping River outside our door disappears beneath a thousand points of candlelight — a tradition found nowhere else in Thailand. This is the story of how it began.

Begin the story

The legend

A Debt Owed to the River

Generations before anyone in Tak had a name for it, families gathered on the riverbank at the end of the rains to give something back. The full moon of the twelfth lunar month was Loi Krathong — a night to honour the Buddha and ask forgiveness from Phra Mae Khongkha, the goddess who lives in every river, canal and rice field. Every krathong sent downstream carried the same quiet thanks, and the same quiet apology, for a year of water taken and water left a little worse.

In the beginning, the krathongs here were sewn from plup phueng leaves — the crinum lily that once grew thick along the Ping. Villagers packed them with tinder soaked in coconut oil, lit them, and let the current carry them into the dark.

How it's made

From Kitchen Scrap to Sacred Light

The lily leaves grew harder to find. What Tak never ran short of was coconut shells — the town has always loved miang kham, a snack built around roasted coconut meat, and every kitchen kept a growing pile of empty shells behind the house. Someone worked out that a single-holed shell, cleaned and dried, floated just as well as a folded leaf. Better, even: it burned longer.

So a household chore became a craft. Villagers melt beeswax saved from temple ordinations into each shell, twist a cotton wick into the shape of a chicken's foot at its centre, and thread the shells together in long chains — krathong sai, "the krathong line." What starts as compost becomes, once a year under the full moon, a small offering of fire.

The festival today

The Night the River Catches Fire

The tradition that once fit in a few village hands now fills the whole riverbank. Every year, eight separate krathong sai processions — each one hundreds of shells long — are lit and released together, until close to a thousand small flames are drifting down the Ping at once.

  • Early November

    Timed to the full moon of the twelfth lunar month — the exact dates move a little each year. Check this year's dates.

  • Krathong Sai Riverside Park

  • Royal lantern procession & fireworks

    Decorated boat parades, folk music contests, and a night market line the riverbank.

  • 8 processions, ~1,000 lights

    Villages and schools each prepare their own krathong sai, launched one after another.

After the lanterns drift on

Some Rooms Look Right At It

The festival ends when the last krathong drifts out of sight — but the river doesn't stop being beautiful. A handful of our rooms face the Rattanakosin Bridge directly, close enough that the lights are still visible long after the crowds have gone home.
